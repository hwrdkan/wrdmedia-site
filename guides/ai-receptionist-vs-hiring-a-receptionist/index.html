AI receptionist vs hiring a receptionist: what each really costs

An AI receptionist is a phone and text assistant that answers calls, qualifies the caller, and books appointments at any hour. Published guides put AI plans at roughly $50 to $300 a month, while a full-time human receptionist in the Dallas area costs roughly $4,000 to $4,700 a month once benefits and overhead are counted. The right answer for a trade business is often both: AI for coverage, a human for complex calls.

What an AI receptionist costs

Most published guides put AI receptionist plans between about $50 and $300 a month, with setup fees that can range from under $100 to several thousand dollars depending on features. Note that most of those guides are written by AI receptionist vendors, so they lean favorable. Self-serve plans are cheap because they are generic. A custom build tied to your calendar, your qualifying questions, and your CRM costs more up front.

For comparison, WRD Media's AI receptionist for Envision clients is $1,500 setup plus $500 a month. See AI phone assistants.

What a human receptionist costs in Dallas-Fort Worth

Base pay is lower in Texas than the national average. Bureau of Labor Statistics figures cited in employer-cost calculators put the May 2025 average for receptionists around $37,750 in Dallas-Fort Worth. The fully loaded cost is higher. One Dallas estimate totals about $56,680 in the first year, which is about $4,723 a month, including an assumed $12,000 for benefits and $4,000 for recruiting and onboarding. Other sources range from about $44,000 to $77,000 a year once taxes, benefits, and training are counted. Part-time at $15 to $20 an hour for 20 hours a week is roughly $1,300 to $1,700 a month, but then calls go to voicemail the rest of the time.

Where each one wins

  • AI wins on nights, weekends, holidays, and busy moments, on consistency, and on cost.
  • A human wins on empathy, sensitive situations, complicated questions, and relationships.
  • A hybrid wins for most trades: the AI answers and books, and hands off to a person when a call needs one.

How to decide

Count how many calls you miss, when, and what a booked job is worth. If you miss calls while on jobs and at night, an AI receptionist pays for itself quickly. If your calls are mostly complex or emotional, put a human first. Our missed calls guide explains which statistics to trust.

Break-even math you can do yourself

Here is an illustration with made-up round numbers, so replace them with your own. Say you miss 20 calls a month while working, and 1 in 10 of those would have become a $6,000 job. That is two jobs, or $12,000 of revenue, a month at risk. If an AI receptionist catches even half, it covers its monthly fee many times over. If you miss two calls a month and your average job is small, it will not. The point is to count your own calls, which our missed calls guide shows how to do in a week.

What to ask any AI receptionist vendor

  • Can I hear real call recordings or read transcripts?
  • Does it connect to my calendar and CRM, or only send me a message?
  • What happens when it does not know the answer?
  • Who sets up and maintains it after launch?
  • What are all the fees: setup, monthly, per minute, and overage?

Common questions

How much does an AI receptionist cost?

Published guides put self-serve plans at roughly $50 to $300 a month. A custom build tied to your calendar and CRM costs more. WRD Media's is $1,500 setup plus $500 a month for Envision clients.

How much does a receptionist cost in Dallas-Fort Worth?

Base pay averages about $37,750 a year per BLS-based figures. Fully loaded, one Dallas estimate is about $56,680 in the first year, roughly $4,700 a month.

Can an AI receptionist replace a human?

For routine calls and after-hours coverage, yes. For complex or sensitive calls, a human is still better, so a handoff matters.

Are the vendor comparisons trustworthy?

Many are written by AI vendors and lean favorable. The wage figures from Bureau of Labor Statistics are more neutral.

How do I know if I need one?

Count how many calls you miss in a month, how many come outside working hours, and what a booked job is worth. If the numbers are small, you may not need one.
